\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}\label{sec:limits}
\subsection{What the evidence supports}\label{sec:supports}
The question was how much of the head's projection a verifier needs. For a greedy or seeded decision the answer the theory gives is exact: an enclosure of every row fine enough to fix the stock kernel's argmax, including its BF16 rounding and its tie rule, and nothing more. The contract that makes this statement true of a real engine names the stock kernel at the same batch shape as the reference and treats that kernel's accumulation error as a modelled assumption, not a fact. On the states the engine actually produced, the evidence an int8 copy of the head supplies is sufficient almost everywhere: one or two rows are re-scored per decision, and the stock kernel is needed for 0.35\% to 1.40\% of rows, depending on which accumulation model is assumed for it. The unmodified engine is not bitwise consistent with itself across batch sizes or between plain and speculative decoding, and the tap names the kernels where the runs part; that is why the reference is the stock kernel at the same batch shape and not an earlier run. What this does not yet show is that the certified path is faster. At the batch sizes the capture used the fallback is rare per batch, but at a serving batch of 128 a whole-batch fallback would be paid in roughly 36--84\% of batches if rows fell back independently, so the runtime result depends on a row-wise fallback and on the stock kernel's row invariance, both pending.

Transport, the mechanism the earlier revisions were built on, failed where it was tested. The weights alone said that its envelope can be the narrower one only if draft and target head inputs agree to within about one part in a hundred; the public DFlash drafter's head inputs differ from the target's by a median 92\% and the native MTP layer's by 95\%, so for every bound family and tiling tested their certificates skip at most 0.65\% of the vocabulary on average, no more than a static screen, and leave sampled acceptance undecided almost always. The negative result is specific: it concerns these two drafters, neither of which is trained to reproduce the target's head input. A drafter trained for that remains open; the drift-scaling extrapolation says a greedy certificate would need about a tenth of the measured drift to become useful and a sampled one less than a hundredth.

\subsection{What did not work}\label{sec:failed}
Several designs failed, each for a reason that limits a tempting argument. Transport failed on both drafters because the draft and target head inputs are far apart, as above (Section~\ref{sec:transport-measured}). A certified decoder tail lost to the certified head alone even under an oracle radius with no overhead, because an int8 surrogate of the final MLP perturbs the head input by about 1\% and can save at most 0.071~GB per token (Section~\ref{sec:tail}). Sharing exact computation across unrelated requests fails in analysis: full-rank differences between requests force private factors, and the one structural candidate, a token-indexed table for layer 0's input projections, has a derived ceiling of about 0.75\% of the dense projection work (Section~\ref{sec:stack-state-need}). FP8 or int4 alone are not certificates on this head: FP8 needs 4.9 candidate rows on average and int4 thousands (Section~\ref{sec:geometry-selfevidence}). The static tile screen, CSV-Decode's mechanism, is looser than a plain per-row bound here, because the 64-row tile radius exceeds the row norm (Table~\ref{tab:headconstants}). On constructed heads, a cheap acceptance decision did not make verification cheap once the dense residual was charged (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}). And among the stack levers, FP8 W8A8 weights gave no consistent gain and an FP8 KV cache none, in first single runs (Section~\ref{sec:stack-levers}).

\subsection{Scope}\label{sec:scope}
The measurements concern one model, one engine commit and one GPU, and the experiments are text-only on a multimodal checkpoint. The agreement of certified tokens with the stock kernel rests on a published model of Hopper tensor-core accumulation and on the absence of reduced-precision split-$K$ reductions in the kernels the engine selects; both are tested, neither is a vendor guarantee. The candidate sets and fallback shares come from offline replay of captured states at the capture run's batch shapes, not from a served run; the fallback shares are for plain decoding, the candidate sets also cover DFlash-4B verify and draft rows, and MTP states are pending. The transport result concerns two drafters, DFlash-4B and the native MTP layer, and the bound families and tilings listed in Section~\ref{sec:transport-measured}. The exact CPU references test the certificates on synthetic heads with small vocabularies and widths; they are tests, not proofs, and the Lean files cover the decision logic and scaled-integer bounds, not IEEE rounding, exponentials or probability. The head constants of Table~\ref{tab:headconstants} use no hidden states, so they decide nothing about certification rates on their own. The byte and ceiling models assume a layer-serial engine, a measured bandwidth that is itself pending, and an assumed tensor-core rate. Every end-to-end result is pending in this revision.

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
A verifier that must agree with a production engine needs less than a full projection, but it needs more than the real-arithmetic winner: it needs to know which token the engine's own kernel, with its rounding and tie rule, would return. Stated that way, the requirement can be met by a low-precision copy of the head on the states this engine produces, with one or two rows re-scored per decision and the stock kernel as a fallback whose frequency was measured on replayed states and whose correctness needs no model. The same refinement provably decides seeded races and fixed-noise verification. The mechanism the earlier revisions were built on, reusing the drafter's work through transported summaries, does not survive the states of either drafter tested, because those states are not close to the target's; a verifier gets usable evidence instead from a smaller copy of its own head. Whether any of this saves time in a served engine is the question the pending measurements answer, and either answer will come with the mechanism that explains it.
