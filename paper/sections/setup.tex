\section{Measurements}\label{sec:measure}
This section measures the certified head on states recorded from the engine, then the served engine with and without engine changes. Its numbers come from six sources, each named with its decision rule where it is used (Table~\ref{tab:sources} in Appendix~\ref{app:register}): the \emph{held-out replay} in FP64, the \emph{kernel replay} at the captured batch shapes, the \emph{engine check} inside SGLang, a head-only \emph{microbenchmark}, the \emph{speculative captures} and the served sweeps (Section~\ref{sec:served}). Numbers labelled baseline are the stock engine's.

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:setup}\label{sec:transport-capture}
\paragraph{System} One NVIDIA GH200 with SGLang~\cite{sglangpaper} at commit \sourcepin{}, PyTorch 2.13 for CUDA 13.0 and the FlashInfer attention backend (Appendix~\ref{app:pins} lists every version). The target is Qwen3.5-4B at revision \modelpin~\cite{qwencard}. The drafters are MTP-4B, the model's native MTP layer (top-$k$ 1; four draft steps in the captures, three in the profile of Figure~\ref{fig:breakdown}), and DFlash-4B, the public block drafter for this target~\cite{dflashpaper,dflash4bcard}, which drafts blocks of 16 tokens and projects them through the target's head.

\paragraph{Capture} A capture-only engine patch records each position's head input as BF16 bit patterns, with the token the engine chose; under speculation it also records the drafter's head inputs and pairs each drafted token with the target head input that verifies it. The capture servers ran without CUDA graphs or the overlap scheduler, with at most 16 running requests in plain decoding (Appendix~\ref{app:transport-capture}). The 320 \emph{capture prompts} come from eight public datasets in four domains and were decoded greedily for 384 tokens. They were split by prompt into an analysis half, on which data-dependent choices were made (such as which columns of $h$ to compute exactly, or a rotation of $h$), and a held-out half. The deployed copy and its bound depend on the weights alone, so the kernel replay need not be held out, and it is not: its positions come from both halves. In plain decoding the FP64 argmax of the captured head input equals the engine's token at 99.51\% of positions, and every disagreement lies within one BF16 spacing of the FP64 winner \ev{alignment}.
\paragraph{A fixed configuration}\label{sec:same-shape} Bit-equality is a useful test only within one configuration, because the stock engine's own output varies across configurations: on one server running at most 16 requests, plain greedy decoding with the client keeping 1 or 32 requests in flight differs within 256 tokens in 52\% of 320 \emph{divergence prompts}, a separate test set (Appendix~\ref{app:divergence}) \ev{state-noise}. A bit-exact trace places the first differences upstream of the head, in kernels whose results depend on the batch or the memory pools, never in the head GEMM or the tie rule \ev{state-mech}. Every comparison here therefore holds the serving configuration fixed and compares a speculative configuration with stock speculative verification, not with plain decoding; the contract also fixes the head's batch shape $M$ (Section~\ref{sec:kernel-fallback}).

\subsection{How Many Entries Are Recomputed}\label{sec:geometry-selfevidence}\label{sec:candidates}
\paragraph{Kernel replay, \Rstock{} rule} On the first 60,000 captured positions, at their engine batch shapes, the certified head recomputes about 1.8 entries per decision on average, and one at the median (Table~\ref{tab:fallback}) \ev{kernel-replay}. The count depends on the stock-kernel model through the margin $\delta_{\max}$; the copy's own bound always uses the conservative constant.

\paragraph{Held-out replay, \Rreal{} rule} This replay of 6,005 held-out plain-decoding positions, drawn as whole decode steps, compares copies of the head on the simpler \Rreal{} decision, whose screen needs no margin; no error bound was violated and the exact winner was always a candidate \ev{selfevidence,selfevidence-csv}. Int8 with one scale per entry reads half the BF16 head's bytes and needs 1.55 candidates on average. FP8 needs about three times as many, and int4 leaves tens of thousands (Figure~\ref{fig:candidates}a; Table~\ref{tab:candidates}). Int8 suffices because of the margins, not because its bound is tight (Section~\ref{sec:envelope}). Its intervals are about two logits wide, while the median gap between the two largest logits is 7.3 logits \ev{stats}, so tightening the bound helps little (Appendix~\ref{app:candidates}). On the verification positions of the speculative captures, the counts are about the same as in plain decoding (Appendix~\ref{app:candidates}) \ev{selfevidence-dflash,selfevidence-mtp}; there only the gap condition of Theorem~\ref{thm:stock} has been evaluated offline.

\subsection{Fallback and Agreement}\label{sec:rstock-share}
\paragraph{Kernel replay, \Rstock{} interval rule} The certified head leaves 0.28\% of positions to the stock head under the \modelhopper{} and 1.46\% under the \modelcons. With that fallback, every token equals the engine's \ev{kernel-replay}. The held-out replay gives nearly the same shares, 0.35\% and 1.40\% (Table~\ref{tab:fallback} in Appendix~\ref{app:candidates}). Under the gap rule its fallback share never falls below about 2\%, because that rule demands a margin of more than one BF16 spacing. This agreement cannot tell the models apart: even an IEEE FP32 summation tree, with a $\gamma_{\rm stock}$ about 360 times smaller than the conservative one, certifies every held-out position and gets every token right \ev{rstock}. It is consistent with each model, not a proof of any; the stock FP32-output kernel's largest observed error, 1/58 of the \modelhopper's bound, is the more direct test (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

Fallback grows with the batch, because one undecided position sends the whole batch to the stock head. If positions fell back independently with probability $p$, a call of $M$ positions would contain an undecided one with probability $1-(1-p)^M$, which matches the measured share of decode steps with a fallback (Table~\ref{tab:fallback}). At $M=128$ the held-out rates predict 36\% and 84\% of calls (Hopper, conservative). On calls of 128 consecutive positions the kernel replay measures 27\% and 77\% (Figure~\ref{fig:candidates}b) \ev{kernel-path}. Independence at the kernel replay's own rates would give 30\% and 85\% (derived) \ev{kernel-replay}, so the measured shares are consistent with undecided positions clustering in fewer calls than independence predicts. With the whole-batch fallback most large batches would rerun the stock head, so at serving batch sizes the certified head depends on the per-position fallback.

\paragraph{Engine check, \Rstock{} interval rule}\label{sec:engine} The engine check ran the kernel inside SGLang, untimed and under the \modelcons, on plain decoding, greedy verification and the drafters' projections. In concurrent runs (up to 16 requests for plain decoding, 8 for speculation) none of 196,734 certified positions differed from the stock head at the same batch shape \ev{kernel-engine}. Runs with one request at a time gave identical certified and stock outputs on all 64 prompts for plain decoding and MTP-4B; DFlash-4B's outputs were not compared end to end. Every path used the whole-batch fallback except one plain-decoding run, which used the per-position fallback. Plain decoding fell back at 1.38\% of positions, close to the kernel replay's 1.46\%, and MTP-4B verification at 1.74\%. DFlash-4B verification sends up to 128 positions per call and left only 3.99\% of them open, yet ran the whole-batch fallback in 90\% of its verify head calls. We infer that the per-position fallback would have to carry the result there; the engine check exercised it only in plain decoding.

\subsection{Head-Only Runtime}\label{sec:kernel-runtime}
The certified head pays off only if
\begin{equation}
T_{\rm pass}+T_{\rm select}+T_{\rm rescore}+P_{\rm fb}(M)\,T_{\rm fallback}(M)<T_{\rm head}(M),\label{eq:headgate}
\end{equation}
where the left side adds the int8 pass, the selection of candidates, their recomputation and the fallback's time weighted by the share $P_{\rm fb}(M)$ of calls that fall back, and $T_{\rm head}$ is the stock head itself: its GEMM, the FP32 copy and the argmax. As a baseline, the stock GEMM at $M=1$ runs at 95\% of the measured read bandwidth \ev{profile-head,profile-bw}, so a pass reading half the bytes needs at least about 170~\textmu s (derived).

In the microbenchmark (the head alone at $M=1$ to 256, CUDA graphs, warm L2 cache) a fully certified batch at $M=1$ takes 239.6~\textmu s, against 370.5~\textmu s for the stock head. Adding each fallback's measured cost at its measured rate gives an expected time of about 0.7 of the stock head's up to 16 positions, with the per-position fallback under the \modelcons{} (Figure~\ref{fig:candidates}c) \ev{kernel-rowwise,kernel-path}. From 128 positions every mode is slower, because the int8 pass alone is slower than the stock head there; with the whole-batch fallback, 77\% of calls also pay for the stock head. The pass is not bound by memory bandwidth: at one position its directed-rounding epilogue keeps the streaming multiprocessors 79\% busy while it draws 62--63\% of DRAM bandwidth, so its time grows with $M$ faster than the stock GEMM's \ev{kernel-micro}. At one position the expected time under the \modelcons{} is 0.66--0.67 of the stock head's with either fallback \ev{kernel-path}. Since the head takes 10.3\% of a plain decode step at batch 1, that would shorten the step by at most about 3.4--3.5\% (derived; the effect on served decoding is not yet measured \pend{bench-cert}).

\subsection{Served Decoding}\label{sec:served}
\paragraph{The stock baseline} Each serving configuration, or \emph{arm}, runs with CUDA graphs and the overlap scheduler (checked at launch) and without the prefix cache, which the workload does not reuse. It is launched once per session at full capacity and swept over client concurrency $c$, the requests a closed-loop client keeps in flight, on 1,152 held-out public prompts (chat, code, mathematics) with 512-token outputs \ev{workload}. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} plots the per-request output rate $x$, including the time to first token, against throughput $y$ (Appendix~\ref{app:serving}). Over three sessions speculation serves more than plain decoding up to $c=32$ and less from 48, for every drafter \ev{bench-frontier}; at $c=1$, DFlash-4B with 16-token blocks, a public drafter that we tuned and validated, gives 3.06 times plain decoding's throughput, both with Triton attention. Every arm is stock or exact up to rounding against a matched stock reference \ev{bench-equality}, and on GSM8K the four arms tested (two MTP, DFlash block 8, replayed plain decoding) are within the noise of plain decoding, excluding losses above about 3.3 points (derived), not smaller ones \ev{bench-quality}.

\paragraph{Engine changes} At low concurrency the largest gain in Table~\ref{tab:levers} comes from host work, not a kernel: making FlashInfer's attention planning, the host work that leaves the MTP cycle idle (Figure~\ref{fig:breakdown}), cheaper and free of blocking GPU reads raises MTP's throughput by 9.4--9.6\% at $c=1$--8 \ev{hostgap}. Stock MTP with Triton attention, which plans nothing on the host, stays faster there (532 against about 500 tokens/s at $c=1$, derived) \ev{bench-frontier}; the patches do not move the MTP frontier. Faster backbone GEMM kernels gain 3.4--3.5\% for plain decoding at $c=1$ and 0.991 times for MTP, whose outputs were not compared \ev{backbone}. Removing per-request state traffic pays as the batch grows: verifying DFlash blocks without per-position state snapshots (SGLang's fold-every-commit protocol) costs 3.2\% at $c=1$ and gains 6.1\% at $c=8$ \ev{drafter-fold}. The certified head on DFlash verification and the composed changes are not yet timed \pend{bench-cert,stack-composition}.
