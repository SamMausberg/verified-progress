\begin{abstract}
A greedy verifier in speculative decoding, like greedy decoding itself, needs one fact from the output head, the winning token, yet the engine scores the whole vocabulary. We find that token from an int8 copy of the head: bound each approximate score's error, recompute exactly the few entries that could still win, and fall back to the engine's own head when the bounds cannot decide. The token must be the one the engine's head returns for a batch of the same size, after BF16 rounding and its tie rule. We prove when bounds on the FP32 sums fix it, given an assumed and tested bound on the closed-source kernel's rounding error, and extend the method to seeded sampling. For Qwen3.5-4B in SGLang on a GH200 the certified head recomputes about 1.8 entries per decision and leaves 0.28--1.46\% of 60,000 replayed positions to the fallback under two error models; every token equals the engine's, also at all 592,433 positions certified in the served engine with the stock head run beside it. Served in three sessions against tuned configurations, it raises throughput at one request by 2.5\% for plain decoding, 4.6\% for MTP and 1.2\% for DFlash, the fastest configuration there, with identical tokens. The fastest configuration loses 1.0--3.0\% at 2 to 8 requests, where its head calls reach 30--64 positions and most verify calls fall back, and 0.6\% at 128, where its calls exceed the head's 64-position limit. At one position, found in a certified run in text after the model's end-of-text token, stock BF16 decoding puts first a token FP32 places 8.9 nats below its top. Bounds carried over from the drafters' heads skip under 0.7\% of the vocabulary in every variant tested: draft and target head inputs differ by a median 92--95\% of the target's norm, where about 1\% is needed.
\end{abstract}
