\begin{abstract}
A greedy verifier in speculative decoding, like greedy decoding, needs only the LM head's winning token, yet the engine scores the whole vocabulary. We ask how little of that work an exact shortcut needs, and what ``exact'' must mean in a production engine. We hold the shortcut to the engine's own head at the same batch size, BF16 rounding and tie rule included, not to exact arithmetic: for Qwen3.5-4B in SGLang on a GH200, the exact winner differs from the engine's token at 0.5\% of greedy positions. A certified head finds that token from an int8 copy of the head: it bounds each score's error, recomputes exactly the few that could still win, and falls back to the engine's head when bounds cannot decide. We prove when bounds on the kernel's FP32 sums fix the token, under an error model of the closed-source kernel's accumulation that is tested, not proved, and extend the proof to seeded sampling. The certified head recomputes about 1.8 entries per decision and leaves 0.28--1.46\% of replayed positions to the fallback under two error models; every token equals the engine head's on the same batch, in replay and while serving. Served against tuned stock configurations in three sessions, it raises throughput by 1.2--4.6\% for a single request with identical tokens, but at two to eight concurrent requests, where most verify calls fall back, the fastest configuration loses; attention, recurrent-state and admission changes gain more. Bounds carried over from the drafters' heads skip almost none of the vocabulary in every variant tested: they beat the int8 copy's only if draft and target head inputs nearly coincide, and the two differ by a median 92--95\% of the target's norm.
\end{abstract}
