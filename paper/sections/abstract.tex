\begin{abstract}
In speculative decoding a target model checks tokens that a cheaper drafter proposes. At each position a greedy verifier, like greedy decoding, needs one fact from the output head, the winning token, yet the engine scores the whole vocabulary. That token can be found more cheaply: approximate every score from an int8 copy of the head, bound each approximation's error, recompute exactly the few entries that could still win, and fall back to the engine's own head when the bounds cannot decide. Inside a production engine the token must be the one the engine's head returns at the same batch shape, after BF16 rounding and its tie rule. We prove when bounds on the head's FP32 sums fix this token, given an assumed bound on its rounding error, and extend the method to seeded sampling. For Qwen3.5-4B in SGLang on a GH200, replaying 60,000 positions captured from greedy decoding, the GPU kernel recomputes about 1.8 entries per decision. It leaves 0.28\% of positions to the stock head under a published model of Hopper accumulation, or 1.46\% under a conservative model, and with that fallback every token equals the engine's. Its expected time for the head alone, from measured times and fallback rates, is about 0.7 of the stock head's up to 16 positions, which would save at most about 3\% of a batch-1 decode step; from 128 positions it is slower, and its effect on serving is not yet measured. In a 64-prompt check inside SGLang under the conservative model no certified token differed from the stock head, but for one public drafter 90\% of verify batches still needed the fallback. Bounds carried over from the drafter's own head computation fail for every variant tested: draft and target head inputs differ by a median 92\% and 95\% for the two public drafters, where about 1\% is needed.
\end{abstract}
