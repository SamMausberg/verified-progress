\begin{abstract}
A greedy verifier in speculative decoding, like greedy decoding itself, needs one fact from the output head, the winning token, yet the engine scores the whole vocabulary. We find that token from an int8 copy of the head: bound each approximate score's error, recompute exactly the few entries that could still win, and fall back to the engine's own head when the bounds cannot decide. The token must be the one the engine's head returns for a batch of the same size, after BF16 rounding and its tie rule. We prove when bounds on the FP32 sums fix it, given an assumed and tested bound on the closed-source kernel's rounding error, and extend the method to seeded sampling. For Qwen3.5-4B in SGLang on a GH200 the kernel recomputes about 1.8 entries per decision and leaves 0.28--1.46\% of 60,000 replayed positions to the fallback, depending on the error model; every token equals the engine's, and none of 592,433 positions certified in the served engine differs. Served in three sessions, it raises throughput at one request by 2.5\% for plain decoding, 4.6\% for MTP and 1.2\% for DFlash, with identical tokens, but the best configuration loses 1.0--3.0\% at 2 to 8 requests, where certified head calls reach 28--64 positions and most verify calls fall back, and 0.6\% at 128, where the head is gated off. The stock engine is itself not FP32-exact: at one position its BF16 decode path commits a token FP32 places 8.9 nats below the top. Among the engine changes we measured, the largest gain at low concurrency came from host work: 9.4--9.6\% for MTP with FlashInfer attention (one session), still short of MTP with Triton attention. Bounds carried over from the drafters' own head computation skip under 0.7\% of the vocabulary in every variant tested: draft and target head inputs differ by a median 92--95\% of the target's norm, where about 1\% is needed.
\end{abstract}
