\begin{abstract}
A greedy verifier in speculative decoding, like greedy decoding, needs only the winning token from the output head, yet the engine scores the whole vocabulary. We find that token from an int8 copy of the head: bound each score's error, recompute exactly the few that could still win, and fall back to the engine's head when the bounds cannot decide. The token must match the engine's head at the same batch size, after BF16 rounding and its tie rule. We prove when bounds on the FP32 sums fix it, assuming a tested bound on the closed-source kernel's rounding error, and extend this to seeded sampling. For Qwen3.5-4B, whose head is 10.3\% of a batch-1 plain decode step in SGLang on a GH200, the certified head recomputes about 1.8 entries per decision and leaves 0.28--1.46\% of 60,000 replayed positions to the fallback under two error models; every token equals the engine's, also at all 592,433 positions certified while serving with the stock head run beside it. Served in three sessions against tuned configurations, it raises throughput at one request by 2.5\% for plain decoding (about 11\% with a free head), 4.6\% for MTP and 1.2\% for DFlash, the fastest there, with identical tokens; the fastest configuration at each of 2 to 8 requests loses 1.0--3.0\% (calls of up to 30--64 positions, most falling back), and 0.6\% at 128 (calls above the head's 64-position limit). FP32 is the wrong reference: at one position after the model's end-of-text token, found in a certified run, stock BF16 decoding at batch 1, unlike transformers', puts first a token 8.9 nats below FP32's top. Bounds carried over from the drafters' heads skip under 0.7\% of the vocabulary in every variant tested: draft and target head inputs differ by a median 92--95\% of the target's norm, where about 1\% is needed.
\end{abstract}
