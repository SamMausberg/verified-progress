\section{Where the time goes, and the serving baseline}\label{app:stack}\label{sec:stack}\label{sec:time}
This appendix measures the stock engine, the baseline a certified head has to improve, and then the engine changes timed against it. A component that occupies a fraction $f$ of a step can speed the step up by at most $1/(1-f)$, so the head's share of the time bounds what any head certificate can deliver. Appendices~\ref{app:bytes} and~\ref{app:profile} measure where the time of a plain decode step and of a speculative cycle goes, and Appendix~\ref{app:serving} says how the stock serving frontier of Section~\ref{sec:served} is measured. Appendix~\ref{app:stack-composition} composes three exact engine changes on DFlash, the certified head among them, and Appendix~\ref{app:cert-served} serves the certified head alone on four tuned arms. The serving protocol in full, what the other layers of the stack could deliver, the first lever measurements and the proposals for writing the recurrent state less often or sharing it across requests are in the research notes (the protocol in their Section~\notessec{notes:serving}{4}, the rest in Section~\notessec{notes:stack}{3}).

\subsection{Bytes per step}\label{app:bytes}\label{sec:time-bytes}
Decode on this GPU streams bytes, so the byte counts of Table~\ref{tab:bytes} give a first picture before any trace. A plain decode step streams every decoder weight once plus the whole head, while the embedding lookup reads one entry per request, so the head is 15.1\% of the weight bytes of a plain step. An MTP draft step runs one small layer followed by the same head, so there the head is 84.1\% of the weight bytes. The recurrent state is per request: the configuration stores it in FP32 (\code{mamba\_ssm\_dtype}), so each request holds 50.3~MB of GDN state, and a decode kernel that reads and writes it once per step moves 100.7~MB per request per step, which equals the weight bytes of a step at a batch of 84 requests.

\subsection{Measured attribution}\label{app:profile}\label{sec:time-measured}\label{sec:time-method}\label{sec:time-head}\label{sec:time-state}
\paragraph{Method} The attribution uses the production configuration (CUDA graphs for prefill and decode, the overlap scheduler, FlashInfer attention, greedy decoding; Appendix~\ref{app:pins}) with plain decoding at batch sizes 1, 8, 32 and 128 and MTP speculation (three steps, top-$k$ 1, four draft tokens, otherwise default flags, not tuned) at 1, 8 and 32. For each batch size $B$ the client holds $B$ streaming requests with chat-templated essay prompts of 26--36 tokens and a budget large enough that none finishes, and a two-second window is profiled once decode has settled, so the batch is exactly $B$ and no prefill falls inside the window; mean contexts in the plain windows were 987, 862, 688 and 418 tokens. Nsight Systems~\cite{nsys} traces each window with node-level CUDA-graph tracing. Each step is reassembled from the correlation identifiers of its graph replays: a plain step runs from one target-graph replay to the next, and a speculative cycle from one draft-graph replay to the next. Kernels are grouped by name, GEMMs are labelled by their neighbours in the replay, and the labels were checked structurally against the per-step counts the model implies (32 MLP gate/up and down, 48 GDN input and 24 output projections, 8 attention projections of each kind and one head per replay). Where kernels on two streams overlap, each instant is split equally among them, so the categories and the idle time sum exactly to the step~\ev{profile-attr}. Every configuration was also measured with no profiler attached, three windows each, which gives the throughput numbers: plain decoding produced 289, 2,048, 6,298 and 14,388 output tokens per second at $B=1$, 8, 32 and 128 (3.46, 3.91, 5.08 and 8.90~ms per step), with a standard deviation of 0.1--0.4\% of the mean across windows. The collected plain steps are within 2.3\% of the uncollected ones, so their kernel shares are reported directly. The bandwidth reference is measured on this GPU: the best of nine launch configurations of a read kernel sustains 3.83~TB/s over 4~GiB (the nine span 3.59--3.83~TB/s), the same configuration reads exactly the head's 1.27~GB at 3.79~TB/s, and the attribution uses the latter as the peak~\ev{profile-bw}.

\paragraph{Plain decoding} Table~\ref{tab:profile-plain} gives the attribution. The head chain takes 10.3\% of a step at $B=1$ and 6.1\% at $B=128$~\ev{profile-head}, below the head's 15.1\% share of the weight bytes, for two reasons visible in the table. The head GEMM is the most efficient kernel in the step: it streams the 1.27~GB weight at 3.3--3.6~TB/s, while the backbone's weight GEMMs reach 1.6--3.2~TB/s, the split-$K$ projections from 4,096 to 2,560 features being the slowest. And the step contains work that does not scale with the weights: the recurrent state, attention, 65 RMSNorm launches and idle time.

\paragraph{The stock head in isolation} Replaying the exact calls SGLang makes with the real tied weight under CUDA graphs, the head GEMM takes 354.5~\textmu s at $M=1$, 362.0~\textmu s at $M=32$ and 569.2~\textmu s at $M=256$, and the whole chain with its FP32 copy and argmax 369.0~\textmu s at $M=1$ (the certified head's microbenchmark, a separate harness, measures the same chain at 370.5~\textmu s~\ev{kernel-path}); up to $M=32$ it runs at 93--95\% of the measured read bandwidth, and reading the BF16 weight once at that bandwidth takes 335~\textmu s. cuBLAS selects an \code{nvjet\_sm90\_tst} kernel whose tile depends on $M$. At $M\ge64$ the FP32 copy and the argmax, each re-reading an $M\times248{,}320$ tensor, add 22--50\% on top of the GEMM (the whole chain takes 558.6~\textmu s at $M=128$ and 850.6~\textmu s at $M=256$). Cold L2 changes the GEMM by under 1~\textmu s up to $M=128$ (5~\textmu s at $M=256$), since the weight is 21 times the 60~MB L2~\ev{profile-head}. This chain is the comparator a certified head must beat, and a kernel that reads the same BF16 bytes has at most a few percent to gain at small $M$. A pass that reads half the bytes therefore needs at least about 170~\textmu s at $M=1$ (derived)~\ev{profile-bw}.

\paragraph{MTP speculation} A cycle replays a draft graph (the MTP layer twice, each time followed by the full head, the FP32 copy and a split-vocabulary top-1), a target-verify graph over four tokens per request, and a draft-extend graph (the MTP layer over the verified tokens and the head once more), with verification and the recurrent-state commit running eagerly between them. Every cycle therefore streams the 1.27~GB head four times. Table~\ref{tab:profile-mtp} gives the attribution. The head takes 17.2\%, 15.3\% and 12.4\% of a cycle at $B=1$, 8 and 32, three quarters of it in the draft heads; because much of the cycle is idle, its share of the time the GPU is busy is larger, 27.5\% at $B=1$~\ev{profile-head}. Most of the idle time is host work in the attention planning of the verify and draft passes~\ev{profile-host}; Section~\notessec{app:levers}{3.6} of the research notes traces it and removes part of it. The profiler inflates this gap, chiefly because each graph launch costs about 350~\textmu s under node-level tracing; subtracting the traced GPU-busy time from the cycle measured without a profiler leaves about 1.4, 1.7 and 1.9~ms per cycle idle (21\%, 20\% and 15\%; derived from two measurements). Without a profiler, MTP produced $1.33\times$, $1.26\times$ and $1.13\times$ the plain throughput at $B=1$, 8 and 32 in these fixed-batch windows, which contain no prefill.

\paragraph{DFlash speculation} The tuned DFlash arm with 16-token blocks, Triton attention for the target and the drafter (Section~\ref{sec:served}), spends its cycle differently~\ev{profile-dflash}. A cycle replays the target-verify graph (every layer over the block, then the head over all 16 block positions) and, after the eager acceptance and state commit, the draft graph (the drafter's six layers, then its projection through the target head over the 15 draft positions), so it streams the head twice. At $B=1$ the verify and draft heads take 4.8\% and 4.7\% of the traced cycle, each a 360~\textmu s GEMM that streams the weight once (Figure~\ref{fig:breakdown}). Over the untraced cycle, itself an estimate, that is 9.3\% (derived), so removing both heads could make the cycle at most $1.10\times$ faster, and a head that read half the weight bytes at most $1.05\times$ (both derived; at most $1.08$--$1.10\times$ at $B=4$ to 64). The target's verify forward takes 66\% of the traced cycle at $B=1$ and 73\% at 64, and two parts of it change with the batch. SGLang's Triton extend-attention kernel runs one thread block per query tile and head, 16 in the target and 32 in the drafter at $B=1$ on a GPU with 132 streaming multiprocessors; with the drafter's share it takes 30\% of the traced cycle at $B=1$ and 14\% at 64. The GDN verify kernel, which writes one FP32 state per block position, takes 36\% of the cycle at $B=64$, and 40\% with the state commit. This arm has no host gap: the host issues each graph launch 0.75 to 1.06 of a cycle before the GPU starts it, and the GPU is busy 96.6--98.8\% of the traced cycle. The tuned arm with 8-token blocks, FlashInfer target attention and FA4 draft attention keeps one: its GPU idles 1.0--1.9~ms per traced cycle outside graph replays at $B=1$ to 64, and at most about 0.66~ms per untraced cycle at $B=1$ (derived, an overestimate). The profile's essay prompts accept only 2.5 to 3.8 tokens per cycle, against 6.2 and 5.0 on the drafter's panel at 16- and 8-token blocks, so the cycle's composition largely carries over (only the state commit and the drafter's KV projection grow with the accepted tokens) but its per-token rates are not the frontier's.

\paragraph{What the attribution decides} Other work finds the head a large share of drafting: FR-Spec profiles EAGLE-2 drafting on Llama-3-8B, with a 128k vocabulary, and finds the head projection at 49\% of draft time and the softmax over its output at a further 13\%~\cite{frspec}, a trimmed-vocabulary study finds the head at 64\% of an EAGLE-3-style drafter's FLOPs~\cite{benshoham2026}, and the compute-optimal vocabulary grows with the compute budget~\cite{tao2024vocab}. On this model and GPU the head is a smaller share of the time than of the bytes: removing the head chain entirely is worth at most $1.11\times$ for plain decoding at $B=1$, $1.07\times$ at $B=128$, $1.21\times$ for the MTP cycle at $B=1$ and $1.10\times$ for the tuned DFlash cycle at $B=1$ (derived from an estimated untraced cycle), before any cost of the certificate. These ceilings are Amdahl bounds for removing a component under otherwise unchanged execution, not predicted gains. The other layers of the stack leave more headroom, and Section~\notessec{notes:ranked}{3.2} of the research notes ranks them.

\subsection{The serving baseline}\label{app:serving}\label{sec:serving}
The stock engine's tuned serving configurations set the bar a certified verifier must clear. Section~\ref{sec:served} reports their frontier (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}) and the engine changes measured against them (Table~\ref{tab:levers}); this subsection gives how the frontier is measured. None of these configurations uses the certified head, which Appendix~\ref{app:cert-served} serves against them.

\paragraph{What the frontier measures}\label{sec:serving-frontiers} Each \emph{arm} is one server configuration, swept over client concurrency in closed loop on a frozen workload of 1,857 public chat, code and mathematics prompts with every output fixed at 512 tokens~\ev{workload}. For request $i$ of the $N$ at a sweep point, with $n_i$ generated tokens, sent at $s_i$ and finished $e_i$ later, the frontier plots
\begin{equation}
x=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{n_i}{e_i},\qquad y=\frac{\sum_in_i}{\max_i(s_i+e_i)-\min_is_i},\label{eq:frontier}
\end{equation}
the mean per-request output rate including the time to first token, and output tokens per second on the one GPU. The arms were chosen on a tuning split of the workload, one launch per configuration~\ev{tuning}, and are reported on a disjoint confirmation split of 1,152 prompts in three sessions, each arm launched afresh beside its matched plain baseline~\ev{bench-frontier}. Every arm is stock or exact up to rounding: each arm that changes the target's arithmetic first diverged from a matched stock reference on the divergence prompts only at an exact tie, within one BF16 step or at a near tie with both margins within two steps, and no output differed in length~\ev{bench-equality}. This class rule was fixed after the first comparisons had been seen, from divergence classes that predate them (Appendix~\ref{app:divergence}). The protocol in full (launch checks, capacity, the configuration search, the output comparisons, a GSM8K quality check and the measurement hazards) is in Section~\notessec{notes:serving}{4} of the research notes.

Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} draws four of the nine arms. At concurrency 1 the DFlash arm with 16-token blocks gives 3.06 times the throughput and 3.45 times the per-request rate of its matched plain baseline; the best speculative arm, MTP, gives 0.98 times plain at concurrency 48 and 0.86 times at 128. Over the 52 points with three or four sessions, the coefficient of variation of throughput has a median of 0.35\% and a maximum of 1.9\%~\ev{bench-frontier}.

\subsection{The composed exact stack}\label{app:stack-composition}
Section~\ref{sec:served} reports the composed result at two concurrencies; Table~\ref{tab:stack-arms} gives every arm. The stack runs on the tuned stock DFlash arm with 16-token blocks of Table~\ref{tab:levers} (Triton attention, at most 64 running requests) and composes three exact engine changes, each of which passed an equality step before any timed run: the snapshot-free GDN verify (F, SGLang's fold-every-commit protocol), the backbone GEMM routing table (G) and the certified head on verification (H: per-position fallback, the \modelcons, at most 64 positions per call). Other exact changes were left out for reasons fixed in advance; the host-gap patches, for one, change nothing on this arm. The composed engine is the pinned SGLang with 33 opt-in patches. S0 is stock SGLang, and B0 the composed engine with every switch off. The arms, their order, the expected ranges and the decision rule were declared before any timed run. Each of three sessions launched S0 and the full stack FGH at its start and end and every other arm once between them, in reversed order in the second session. An arm's session ratio is over the mean of S0's two launches, and an interval that excludes 1 decides a speedup or a slowdown~\ev{stack-composition}.

B0 is bitwise equal to S0 (token identifiers and top-five log-probabilities), and F to B0. G, FG and FGH are exact up to rounding against stock DFlash with 16-token blocks: every first divergence is an exact tie, one BF16 step or a near tie. H gave the same tokens as B0, FGH the same as FG, and in check mode none of 249,392 and 250,464 certified verify positions differed from the stock head. All of these were classified in the equality step, on 320 prompts of 256 tokens at $c=1$ with a running limit of 4; the timed sessions, at $c$ up to 8 with a running limit of 64, did not compare outputs.

Against S0 the full stack loses at low concurrency and gains at $c=8$. Its per-request rate is 0.986 times S0's at $c=1$ and 0.972 times at $c=2$, then 1.013 times at $c=4$ and 1.079 times at $c=8$, where throughput is 1.073 times; all are decided. It lies inside its declared range only at $c=1$ and below it at $c=2$, 4 and 8. F's three sessions agree with its one-session measurement in Table~\ref{tab:levers} (throughput 0.971 and 1.068 times at $c=1$ and 8, against 0.968 and 1.061). Part of G's change is not GEMM time: G rounds differently, so the token trajectories change, and the tokens accepted per verify cycle rise by 0.7\% at $c=1$ and fall by 0.4\% at $c=2$, the same in every session. B0 shows no detectable cost of carrying the patches.

H alone never served a request. In each session its server ran out of memory during start-up. The certified head's work buffers inside the verify CUDA graphs, which the engine's memory sizing does not see, made those graphs take 6.13--6.25~GB against 2.91~GB for S0, and beside the stock verify's 48.75~GB per-position state cache for 64 requests too little memory remained for DFlash's sampling warm-up. F removes that cache, so FGH started, with 13.4--13.6~GB free after its graph captures. By the declared rule H's own ratio is void, and the certified head's effect on DFlash alone stays unmeasured here; Appendix~\ref{app:cert-served} measures it with the attention cache pinned. Within each session FGH against FG gives $+1.1\%$ in per-request rate at $c=1$, $+0.1\%$ at $c=2$, $-0.7\%$ at $c=4$ (below 1 in every session) and $-0.3\%$ at $c=8$. That is a reading from an unbalanced order, neither declared nor a test. By H's 64-position limit, most verify calls at $c=8$, of 128 positions, would run the stock head; the sessions recorded no head counters.

Derived bounds put these ratios in proportion. An engine running this drafter at the bandwidth floor, with no host idle and no per-position state, would have at most 1.81 times the tuned stock arm's per-request decode rate at $c=1$ and 3.15 times at $c=8$, at the measured 5.7--5.8 tokens per verify cycle (derived); the composed changes give 0.986 and 1.079 times S0's per-request rate there. That most of what remains is a drafting limit is an inference from these bounds, not a measurement. The stack ran on 16-token blocks only. At $c=8$ the DFlash arm with the higher throughput uses 8-token blocks, where F alone gave 1.058 times in one session~\ev{drafter-fold}; no composed stack was timed there, and FGH's throughput at $c=8$, 3,749 tokens/s, exceeds that arm's 3,651 only across sessions, without an interval.

\subsection{The certified head, served}\label{app:cert-served}

Each pair ran in three sessions, certified first in the second. The head runs in the microbenchmark's best mode where it pays: the per-position fallback, the \modelcons{}, and certification only for calls of at most 64 positions; a larger call replays the same CUDA graph with the head gated off. Both DFlash arms pin their attention cache at 60,000 tokens, because the certified graphs' work buffers are allocated after SGLang sizes its pools (unpinned, the composed stack's certified server ran out of memory, Appendix~\ref{app:stack-composition}). None of the 132 timed points is invalid. Table~\ref{tab:cert-served} gives every point.

\paragraph{Throughput} By the declared rule the certified head raises throughput at $c=1$ by 2.5\% for plain decoding, 4.6\% for MTP and 1.2\% for 16-token DFlash, and lowers 8-token DFlash's by 1.2\% at $c=4$, where the decision rests on Holm's last step at the nominal 0.05 and the Bonferroni interval across the four primaries includes 1. The gains are confined to small batches and fall short of the prediction. Plain decoding gains about 2\% up to $c=16$ and 1.1\% at 32, then nothing at 64. MTP gains 3.0--4.6\% up to $c=4$ and 1.7\% at 8, shows no detectable change at 16 and 32, and loses 3.3\% at 64, where its certified calls are draft calls of 64 positions. Both DFlash arms lose 1.0--3.0\% at every tested point where the head is active above $c=1$, where their certified calls reach 28--64 positions. On the served envelope, the confirmation frontier's best arm at each concurrency (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}), the ratio is 1.012 at $c=1$, 0.990, 0.970 and 0.975 at $c=2$, 4 and 8, and 0.994 at 128, with no detectable change at 16, 32 and 64: the served envelope rises only at $c=1$ and falls at $c=2$, 4, 8 and 128. Where every certified path is gated off, the captured certified graph still costs 0.6\% (plain decoding at $c=128$) to 1.2\% (16-token DFlash at $c=8$), and the certified graphs take 3.8--25.7~GB more memory at capture. The check launches show the share of head calls that need a fallback growing with the batch, from 2\% at $c=1$ to 61\% at 64 for plain decoding and to 76--82\% at $c=4$ for 16-token DFlash. That is consistent with the losses, but the time per fallback inside the served graphs was not measured, so the mechanism is a hypothesis.

\paragraph{Exactness} Check launches ran each certified arm at its tuned flags with the stock head also computed in every certified step, counting on the device the positions whose token differs: every certified call was counted, and 0 of 592,433 certified positions differ. At $c=1$, where one request at a time gives both arms the same batches, every request's token ids were identical between the arms in all three sessions (plain decoding, MTP and 16-token DFlash, 32 of 32 prompts each); 8-token DFlash has no $c=1$ point and rests on check mode alone. A batch's verify goes to the stock head if any of its requests asks for log-probabilities, penalties, a grammar or token mask, logit bias, a custom logit processor or logprob capture. The benchmark workload has none of these. In a deployment, a batch that holds such a request gets the stock verify: a condition on both the exactness scope and the speedup.

Above $c=1$ closed-loop batches vary from run to run. Pooled over $c>1$, first divergences between the certified and stock runs of a session occur at 1.21 times (95\% interval 0.92--1.59) the rate between stock runs of different sessions for plain decoding, 1.11 times (1.0002--1.22) for MTP and 1.03 times (0.91--1.16) for 8-token DFlash; 16-token DFlash diverged 9 times against none between stock runs, on the same 3 prompts at $c=8$ in every session, and a wave control points to the batch evolution as the cause. Re-scored on a stock server at batch 1, all but 20 of the 1,113 first-divergence contexts are exact ties or one BF16 step apart (19 within 0.5 nats, 1 large). MTP's lower bound sits just above 1, and the excess fits the certified engine's different step times changing the batch evolution: under identical batch evolution the arms are identical. In a seeded control at the most sensitive position known (prompt \code{579ae7ce}, decoded with MTP from output position 400 in waves of 1, 8, 12 and 16 requests), the certified verify decided all 3,708 verify steps and every token of the 109 requests equalled stock's on both passes. With log-probabilities requested, which keep the verify on the stock head, the certified graphs, the certified draft and draft-extend heads and the gated-off verify left every token of the 109 requests equal to stock's, and the top-five and five tracked tokens' log-probabilities at position 439 identical to the bit. Waves with fixed batch evolution also gave identical outputs for MTP (64 of 64 prompts, and 512 of 512 in waves of 64 at the timed pools) and for 16-token DFlash at $c=8$ with the head gated off (64 of 64).

\paragraph{The one large divergence} Session 1's certified MTP run at $c=64$ committed token 1756 at prompt \code{579ae7ce}, output position 439, a single position in text the model wrote after its own end-of-text token; stock runs there committed 68189, 8078 or 5715. At that position an FP32 forward puts 68189 on top and 1756 8.9 nats below it, and FP32's two paths (one forward, and recurrent decoding) agree within 0.0001 nats and side with the stock BF16 prefills, while stock batch-1 plain decoding from position 400 puts 1756 on top. By the reading of these paths fixed before the comparison ran, the stock decode path carries the error there, a stock-engine numerics fault independent of the certified head; the layer that errs was not located. As an interpretation, the position is ill-conditioned in BF16: every path, BF16 and FP32, agrees within 0.05 nats on each of the 39 positions before it, and they part at the position alone. Against FP32, closed-loop runs at $c=64$ commit a token at least 2 nats below the top there in 4 of 19 stock MTP draws and in 8 of 34 uninstrumented draws of engines carrying the certified graphs, a grouping chosen after seeing the data; 1756 itself appears only in the latter (4 of 34 against 0 of 19; post hoc, one-sided Fisher $p$ about 0.16), so the arms differ in which wrong token, not measurably in how often. No observation requires an error in the certified head: the certified graphs with the head never deciding committed 1756 too, the head matched the stock argmax at every check-mode position and at all 967,680 positions of a stress test (batch-1 inputs in head-only graphs), and stock batch-1 plain decoding itself puts 1756 on top. The certified head's contract is identity with the stock engine's output for the same batch, and the stock engine is not FP32-exact at positions like this one.
